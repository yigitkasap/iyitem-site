    "q": "What is an MRI examination?",
    "a": "MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) is an imaging method developed by placing the body in a strong magnetic field so that the magnetic vectors of its hydrogen atoms align north and south. Hydrogen is used for imaging because it is abundant in the body's fat and water and because it is a simple atom made up of a single proton and electron. Radiofrequency pulses applied at various angles change the direction of these magnetic vectors, and when the pulse stops the vector returns to the direction of the magnetic field. In the process, the hydrogen in each tissue releases a different amount of energy. That energy is measured by devices called coils and converted into an image, so differences between tissues become visible. Because the amount of hydrogen — and the energy released by its vectors — differs between liver tissue, fat, pancreas, brain tissue and water-filled structures such as the bladder, these can be told apart. Applying the radiofrequency wave from various angles and for different lengths of time produces a range of different images. These are what make up MRI sequences."

    "q": "How is an MRI performed, and how long does it take?",
    "a": "To generate a high magnetic field, MRI scanners take the form of a metal tube roughly 1.8 to 2 metres long with a 60 or 70 centimetre opening through the middle. On newer scanners this opening has been widened to 70 centimetres, which makes for a more comfortable environment for claustrophobic patients. The most homogeneous and strongest magnetic field is found exactly in the centre of the bore, so the region to be scanned is positioned there. The patient lies on the MRI table and is positioned so that the region to be imaged sits at the centre of the scanner. Signal receivers called coils are wrapped around that region. Depending on the region, the nature of the condition and the requests of the patient's doctor, the scan generally takes between 10 and 45 minutes. Where necessary, contrast is given intravenously. During the scan, images of the region are acquired in all three planes with various sequences, each taking roughly 2–3 minutes. It is possible to shorten this, but MRI sequences have an optimum duration: shorten it and image quality suffers; lengthen it too far and patient movement can degrade the images. Artificial intelligence has shortened scan times. The patient must remain completely still throughout. For comfort, there is the option of watching television through a mirror or listening to music. An open MRI scanner has metal above and below the patient and is open at the sides, but because it cannot reach a high field strength it does not produce images as quickly or as well as 1.5 and 3 tesla scanners."

    "q": "What is the difference between CT and MRI?",
    "a": "In computed tomography, X-rays from a source pass through the body lying inside the bore and reach a detector on the other side. The information gathered about the body's tissues is then turned into an image by computer. The X-ray source and detector rotate around the bore, scanning the tissues. In MRI, by contrast, the direction of the magnetic fields of the hydrogen atoms in the body placed inside a magnetised bore is changed by radio waves. The energy they then release is picked up by receivers called coils and converted into an image. MRI uses no radiation; CT does. MRI builds its image by distinguishing tissues such as fat, water, soft tissue, muscle, blood and bone according to differences in the amount of hydrogen in them. CT distinguishes tissue structure according to how easily X-rays pass through it. Air-filled organs such as the lungs and paranasal sinuses are better assessed with CT, as are fine bone fractures. Moving organs such as the coronary arteries are better assessed with a fast CT scan than with MRI. The bowel, likewise a moving organ, is better assessed with CT, particularly when filled with contrast. For all other organs, MRI gives more detailed information. Specialised studies such as MR spectroscopy, MR tractography and MR neurography cannot be performed with CT. The absence of radiation and of any known side effect is a major advantage of MRI: it can be performed on pregnant women and on babies in the womb. Its disadvantage is that scans take longer — CT is generally over in 10–15 seconds, whereas MRI takes 10 to 45 minutes. The MRI bore is longer, and the gradients that produce the changes in magnetic field are audible during the scan. Newer scanners are quieter, but the noise remains. The CT bore is shorter and there is no noise. The contrast that can be given intravenously in MRI is less allergenic; allergy is more common with CT contrast. The walls of a CT room are lined with lead to stop X-rays leaving the room, while the walls of an MRI room contain a copper cage to shield the scanner's magnetic field from metal outside."

    "q": "How is an MRI of the hand performed?",
    "a": "An MRI scanner is a large magnet, and the field is most homogeneous exactly in the centre of the bore. Whichever region is being scanned therefore has to be brought to, or close to, that centre. A component called a coil, which collects the MRI signals, is wrapped around the region being imaged; it picks up the magnetic information from the body and passes it to the computer. For an MRI of the hand, the patient lies face down with the arm extended forward in a “superman” position, the coil is wrapped around the hand, and the scan is performed that way. If the patient cannot tolerate this position, the scan can be done with the arm at the side, but this is not preferred, as the region being imaged cannot then be brought to the centre of the scanner."

    "q": "What does an MRI look at? Does it show everything?",
    "a": "MRI can image every region of the body and allows both soft tissue and bone to be examined in detail. Conditions of the brain, spine and spinal cord, and assessment of muscles, of the nerves within body tissue, of the meniscus and ligaments in the knee, of tendons, and of the ligaments and cartilage in the shoulder and other joints, are all clearer with MRI than with other techniques. The abdominal organs are also better assessed with MRI. MRI is, however, an imaging technique: it does not show functional disorders or metabolic values — that is, biochemical or microbiological findings. Every technique has its own strengths. Computed tomography, for instance, shows lung parenchyma better. Narrowing or blockage in the coronary arteries is better shown by computed tomography and DSA angiography. The gallbladder is more easily and reliably assessed with ultrasound. Older, small bone fractures are better assessed with X-ray and computed tomography."

    "q": "CT or MRI? What can be used instead of MRI?",
    "a": "Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are quite different imaging techniques, each with strengths and shortcomings, though in some regions one can be used in place of the other. CT is superior to MRI for examining the paranasal sinuses and the thorax (lungs), for imaging small, fine cortical bone fractures, and for imaging many conditions of the temporal bone. For some abdominal conditions the two are interchangeable. MRI cannot be performed on people with a pacemaker, or on patients with a metallic foreign body in a vital organ; CT is used instead.
